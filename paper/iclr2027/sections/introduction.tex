\section{Introduction}
\label{sec:introduction}
\input{figures/intro_teaser}

As people increasingly use large language models (LLMs) to support decisions, they need to know what information the models rely on. Input attribution helps answer this question by tracing a response back to the words in its context. A reasoning response depends on both the information available and the computations that select and combine it \citep{ferrando2024information}. Figure~\ref{fig:overview}(d) shows a passage containing a playwright, William Shakespeare, and a composer, William Walton. The names supply candidate answers, and the phrase ``the author of the play'' specifies which role to use. Attribution should account for how both the candidate names and the requested role affect the response. It should also distinguish words that support the response from words that oppose it.

This paper studies how masking selected prompt tokens would change the model's score for a chosen response span. The reference masks these source tokens with an end-of-sequence (EOS) token at the same positions. Both executions score the same target span using the fixed response prefix. The target can be an answer span or the full reasoning and answer. We assign each source token a signed share of the resulting log-probability difference, so the contributions add across words or passages.

Attribution methods can explain prediction differences relative to a reference \citep{sundararajan2017axiomatic,shrikumar2017deeplift}. Relevance propagation and representation decomposition trace information through attention and recurrent states \citep{ali2022conservative,achtibat2024attnlrp,jafari2024mambalrp,modarressi2023decompx,pitorro2025latim}. FlashTrace follows source importance through multi-token responses \citep{pan2026flashtrace}. For the fixed-response question above, the target is a finite log-probability difference. Computing token contributions requires relating the response-score difference to activation differences throughout the model.

Several inputs to the same operation can change together, so assigning the response-score difference requires accounting for their combined effect. An attention product receives changing value vectors and attention weights. Gated DeltaNet receives changing values, keys, queries, and gates that affect its memory updates and reads \citep{yang2024gated}.

\dt{} modifies backpropagation because derivatives evaluated at the original execution generally cannot reconstruct the finite response-score change caused by masking. From each operation's formula and the two executions, it constructs local finite-change rules and composes them in one reverse pass into effective secant slopes from input embeddings to the response score. Just as a secant slope converts horizontal displacement into vertical displacement, multiplying each embedding component's change by its effective slope gives its signed share of the score change. Summing these shares within a token gives its attribution; local conservation makes the total across all tokens equal the measured difference and preserves this interpretation when tokens are grouped into sentences or passages. Figure~\ref{fig:overview}(a) shows the complete method, while (b) and (c) illustrate its backward calculations for two operations.

\paragraph{Contributions.}
\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{Signed evidence for the reasoning and answer together.} DT combines a source token's effects across response positions into one contribution to the full response's score difference. The signed scores identify support and opposition and can be summed to explain sentences or passages (Section~\ref{sec:reverse}).
\item \textbf{One procedure for attention and linear attention.} We derive backward calculations for attention products, normalization, and Gated DeltaNet as examples of the operations handled by \dt{}. These calculations account for simultaneous changes in their inputs, allowing the same attribution procedure to follow attention and recurrent memory (Sections~\ref{sec:attention}--\ref{sec:memory}).
\item \textbf{Auxiliary storage linear in sequence length.} FlashAttention-style tiles \citep{dao2022flashattention} and Flash Linear Attention (FLA) chunks \citep{yang2024fla} bound the workspace used for intermediate products. At fixed model dimensions and chunk size, longer inputs and responses require only linear growth in auxiliary storage (Section~\ref{sec:computation}).
\end{itemize}

On Qwen3-8B retrieval and reasoning tasks, \dt{} achieves the lowest deletion-faithfulness scores on most tasks and the highest evidence recovery across the six needle-in-a-haystack tasks (Section~\ref{sec:evaluation}). On Qwen3.5-9B, \dt{} lowers RISE on 10 of 13 tasks and MAS on 12 relative to FlashTrace.
